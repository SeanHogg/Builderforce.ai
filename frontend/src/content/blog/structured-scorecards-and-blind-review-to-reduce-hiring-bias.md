# Structured Scorecards and Blind Review: How to Reduce Hiring Bias

## Why Unstructured Interviews Mislead You

The unstructured interview — a free-flowing chat that ends in a thumbs up or down — is one of the worst-performing tools in hiring, and one of the most popular. It measures rapport, similarity, and confidence far more than it measures competence. It's also where bias hides best: when criteria are implicit, "culture fit" becomes a euphemism for "reminds me of me."

Structured scorecards fix this by making the criteria explicit, identical across candidates, and recorded. You stop comparing vibes and start comparing evidence on the same axes.

## Step 1: Define the Dimensions Before the First Interview

Before you talk to anyone, decide what the role actually requires and turn it into 4–6 scoreable dimensions — for example: problem-solving, role-specific skill, collaboration, communication. For each dimension, write what a 1 looks like and what a 5 looks like, so two interviewers reading the same answer land on a similar score.

Define these once per role and reuse them for every candidate. The moment the criteria are fixed up front, you've removed the biggest source of inconsistency: each interviewer inventing their own bar mid-conversation.

## Step 2: Ask Every Candidate the Same Core Questions

Structure means consistency. Each interviewer covers the same core questions for their assigned dimensions, so candidates are compared on the same evidence rather than on whatever happened to come up. You can still follow threads — but everyone hits the same anchor questions.

This is what makes scores comparable. If candidate A was asked about a hard trade-off and candidate B wasn't, their "judgment" scores aren't measuring the same thing. Same questions, same rubric, comparable scores.

## Step 3: Review Blind Where You Can

Bias often enters before the interview even starts — from a name, a photo, a school. Strip identifying details from work samples and written submissions so reviewers score the work, not the person. For live interviews, full blinding isn't possible, but you can still anonymize take-home reviews and resume screens.

Blind review is most powerful on the artifacts: code samples, writing tests, design exercises. Score the artifact against the rubric first, attach the name second.

## Step 4: Score Independently, Then Aggregate

Have interviewers record their scores on the [scorecard](/workforce) *before* the debrief, independently. Anchoring is real: the first loud opinion in a room drags everyone toward it. Independent scores first, discussion second.

Then aggregate. Look at the scores across dimensions and across interviewers, and make the decision on the pattern in the evidence — not on who argued hardest in the room. A candidate who scores consistently well across independent reviewers on the dimensions that matter is a far safer bet than one who won a debate. Structured, recorded, aggregated decisions are also your best defense if a hiring decision is ever questioned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a structured scorecard?

A pre-defined set of dimensions for a role — each with explicit rating levels — that every interviewer scores every candidate against. It replaces a gut-feel thumbs up/down with consistent, comparable evidence recorded the same way for everyone.

### Does blind review work for live interviews?

Full blinding isn't possible once you're face to face, but you can anonymize the artifacts — take-home submissions, code samples, writing tests, resume screens — and score those against the rubric before attaching a name. That's where blind review has the most leverage.

### Why score before the debrief?

To avoid anchoring. The first strong opinion in a debrief pulls everyone toward it. Recording independent scores first, then discussing, keeps each interviewer's read intact so the aggregate reflects real consensus rather than the loudest voice.
