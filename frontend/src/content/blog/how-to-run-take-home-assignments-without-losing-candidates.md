# How to Run Take-Home Assignments Without Losing Good Candidates

## Why Take-Homes Win — and Where They Backfire

Work samples are among the most predictive hiring signals there is: watching someone do a slice of the actual job beats inferring it from a conversation. But take-homes backfire when they're disrespectful of the candidate's time — a 10-hour unpaid project from a strong candidate with three offers in flight is a project they'll simply decline.

The fix isn't to abandon take-homes; it's to scope them honestly and evaluate them rigorously. A two-hour, tightly-scoped assignment scored against a rubric gives you the predictive signal without the candidate attrition.

## Rule 1: Scope to Two Hours (and Pay for More)

Design the assignment to take a focused candidate about two hours, and say so in the brief. State a time box explicitly: "Spend no more than two hours; we evaluate what you can do in that window, not how many hours you're willing to give us."

If the role genuinely needs a deeper sample, pay for it. A paid project converts a take-home from an imposition into a paid trial, and the candidates worth hiring respond well to being valued. The unpaid 10-hour project is the single biggest reason strong candidates drop out of pipelines.

## Rule 2: Make It Resemble the Real Job

The assignment should be a slice of the work the person will actually do — not a brain-teaser, not a trick. For an engineer, a small bug fix or feature in a representative codebase. For a marketer, a campaign brief for a real (or realistic) product. For a designer, a constrained redesign with a stated goal.

Resemblance is what makes the sample predictive. The closer the take-home is to day-one work, the more its result tells you about day-one performance — and the more candidates feel the process was fair.

## Rule 3: Score with a Rubric, Before You See the Name

Decide what good looks like *before* you read a single submission. Write a rubric with the dimensions you care about — correctness, clarity, judgment, communication — and the levels for each. Then score every submission against that same rubric.

Where possible, review blind: strip the candidate's name and identifying details so you're scoring the work, not the person. ## Rule 4: Close the Loop With Feedback

Candidates invest real effort in a take-home. The minimum return is a clear, timely decision; the gold standard is a sentence of specific feedback. It costs you little and it's the difference between a rejected candidate who badmouths your process and one who refers a friend next quarter.

Feedback also forces discipline on your side: if you can't articulate why a submission scored where it did, your rubric wasn't specific enough. Closing the loop makes the next round of assignments sharper.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a take-home assignment take?

Design it for about two focused hours and state that time box in the brief. If the role truly needs a deeper sample, pay the candidate for their time. Long unpaid take-homes are the leading cause of strong candidates dropping out of a pipeline.

### How do I keep take-home evaluation fair?

Write a rubric before you read any submissions, score every candidate against the same dimensions, and review blind where you can by stripping names and identifying details. Recording structured scores on a scorecard keeps every reviewer on the same criteria.

### Should take-homes look like the real job?

Yes. The most predictive assignments are a representative slice of day-one work, not abstract puzzles. Resemblance to the real job both improves the signal and makes candidates feel the process is fair.
