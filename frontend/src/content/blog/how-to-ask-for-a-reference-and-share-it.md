# How to Ask for a Reference (and Share It So It Counts)

## Who to Ask — and Who Not To

The best reference is someone who managed you closely, recently, and can speak to specific results — a former direct manager beats a skip-level, who beats a peer, who beats a title-impressive name who barely worked with you. Recency and proximity matter more than seniority. A recent manager who can describe exactly what you shipped is worth more than a VP who vaguely remembers you.

Avoid: anyone you're unsure will be enthusiastic, anyone you parted with on bad terms, and your current manager if your search is confidential. A lukewarm reference is worse than one fewer reference.

## How to Ask (the Right Way)

Ask early, ask directly, and make it easy to say yes — or no. "Would you be comfortable being a *strong* reference for me?" gives them a graceful out if they're hesitant, and you want that out: a reluctant yes becomes a tepid call.

When they agree, hand them what they need: the roles you're targeting, a short reminder of the work you did together and the results, and the timing. A reference who's reminded of the specific project you partnered on gives a far more compelling endorsement than one reaching back through a foggy memory.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who makes the best reference?

Someone who managed you closely and recently and can speak to specific results — usually a former direct manager. Recency and proximity matter more than seniority; a recent manager who can describe what you shipped beats a senior name who barely worked with you.

### How should I ask someone to be a reference?

Ask early and directly, and phrase it so they can decline gracefully — 'would you be comfortable being a strong reference for me?' Then make it easy: remind them of the work you did together, the results, and the roles you're targeting so their endorsement is specific.
