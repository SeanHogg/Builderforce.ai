# Retained vs Contingency Search — and Why Warm Intros Beat Both

## The Two Classic Models

**Contingency search** means the recruiter is paid only if their candidate is hired — typically a percentage of first-year salary, collected on placement. It's low-commitment for the employer: no placement, no fee. Recruiters work many roles at once and move fast on the easy-to-fill ones.

**Retained search** means the employer pays the recruiter in installments to run a dedicated search, hired or not — usually a third up front, a third at a milestone, a third on placement. The recruiter commits real time to a single role and works it exhaustively. Retained is the model for senior, scarce, or confidential roles.

## When to Use Contingency

Contingency fits roles with a deep, accessible talent pool and a clear spec — individual contributors, mid-level managers, high-volume hiring. You're paying for reach and speed, and you only pay on success, so the downside is capped.

The trade-off: a contingency recruiter juggling 20 roles gives each one a fraction of their attention, and races other recruiters to submit the same candidates. You get breadth and speed, not depth. For roles where any qualified hire is a good hire, that's exactly the right trade.

## When to Use Retained

Retained fits roles where a bad hire is expensive and a long vacancy is worse — executives, rare specialists, confidential replacements. Because the recruiter is paid to run the search regardless of outcome, they invest in mapping the whole market, approaching passive candidates, and managing a discreet process.

The up-front fee aligns incentives: the recruiter isn't racing to submit fast, they're working to find the *right* person. For a VP or a niche technical leader, paying for that depth beats hoping a contingency recruiter happens to surface the one candidate who fits.

## Why Warm Intros Outperform Both

For the roles that matter most, the highest-converting source isn't either fee model — it's a warm introduction. A candidate introduced by someone they trust arrives pre-vetted, more responsive, and more likely to accept. Warm intros convert at multiples of cold outreach and shorten the trust-building that normally takes several rounds.

The practical move is to mine your existing network before (or alongside) engaging a search firm: who do your strong employees know? Who has a former colleague who'd be perfect? A single warm intro to the right person can outperform weeks of either retained or contingency sourcing — and it costs you nothing but the ask.

## Combine the Models

These aren't mutually exclusive. A common high-performing pattern: work your warm network for the obvious fits, run contingency for the breadth roles, and reserve retained for the one or two searches where depth and discretion are non-negotiable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the core difference between retained and contingency search?

Payment timing and commitment. Contingency pays the recruiter only on a successful placement, so they work many roles fast. Retained pays in installments to run a dedicated search whether or not it results in a hire, so the recruiter commits deep attention to one role — the model for senior, scarce, or confidential positions.

### Why do warm introductions convert better than cold outreach?

Trust transfers. A candidate introduced by someone they already trust is more responsive, arrives pre-vetted, and is more likely to accept. That shortcuts several rounds of trust-building, which is why a single warm intro often outperforms weeks of cold sourcing for high-stakes roles.

### Can I use more than one model at once?

Yes, and most effective teams do — warm network for the obvious fits, contingency for breadth roles, and retained for the one or two searches where depth and discretion are essential.
