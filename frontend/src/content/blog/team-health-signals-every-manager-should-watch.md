# Team Health Signals Every Manager Should Watch (Before People Quit)

## Resignations Are Lagging Indicators

By the time someone gives notice, the decision was made weeks or months earlier. The resignation is a lagging indicator; the signals that predicted it were visible all along. The managers who keep their best people aren't luckier — they read the leading indicators and act while there's still something to act on.

The goal of a team-health view isn't a vanity dashboard. It's to surface the quiet drift — the things that don't show up in a status report — early enough to do something about it.

## Signal 1: Engagement Drift

The clearest early signal is a change in someone's normal pattern. A person who used to volunteer ideas going quiet. A consistently positive report whose tone flattens. Participation tailing off in places they used to show up. None of these is damning on its own — but a sustained drop from someone's own baseline is the canary.

Watch the *delta*, not the absolute level. A naturally reserved person being reserved is nothing; a normally vocal person going silent is everything. Baseline-relative reads are what separate signal from noise.

## Signal 2: 1:1 and Goal Stalls

Two concrete, trackable signals: cancelled 1:1s and stalled goals. When a report starts pushing or skipping 1:1s — or when you do — the most important conversation channel is closing exactly when it matters. And a goal that hasn't moved in weeks is either blocked, deprioritized, or abandoned, and any of those is worth a conversation.

Because Builderforce ties 1:1s, goals, and the roster together, the [team health](/insights/space) view can surface these as structured signals rather than something you have to notice by accident across five different tools.

## Signal 3: Workload and Recognition Imbalance

Two imbalances quietly burn people out. The first is workload: the person who's always the one carrying the launch, covering the on-call, absorbing the overflow. The second is recognition: high contribution that goes unacknowledged. Either one, sustained, leads to resentment and then departure.

These are harder to see from a status report and easier to see from patterns over time — who's consistently overcommitted, whose wins aren't being named. A team-health view that aggregates over weeks makes the pattern visible while you can still rebalance.

## From Signal to Action

A signal is only useful if it changes what you do. When the team-health view flags a person, the move is almost always the same: a real conversation in the next 1:1. Name what you're seeing, ask open questions, and listen. Most of the time the problem is addressable — a blocker you can clear, a recognition gap you can close, a workload you can rebalance — *if* you catch it early.

The managers who retain their teams treat the team-health view as a prompt to have conversations, not a scoreboard. The dashboard's only job is to make sure the conversation happens before the resignation letter does.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the earliest signs someone might leave?

Changes from a person's own baseline: a normally vocal report going quiet, flattening tone, cancelled 1:1s, and stalled goals. These leading indicators show up weeks before a resignation, which is itself a lagging indicator of a decision already made.

### Why watch the delta instead of the absolute level?

Because people differ. A naturally reserved person being reserved is noise; a normally vocal person going silent is signal. Reading each person relative to their own baseline is what separates a real warning from a personality trait.

### What should I do when the team-health view flags someone?

Have a real conversation in the next 1:1 — name what you're seeing, ask open questions, and listen. The view is a prompt to talk early, not a scoreboard. Most issues are addressable if you catch them before the decision to leave is made.
